2Motivation

Who has the problem

The first person with this problem is the author, a Yale SOM MBA student recruiting for a Summer 2027 internship, who first built a private tracker for himself; this app rebuilds it as a separate product any student can use, with none of his data. He is not alone: MBA students apply to many organisations at once, each with its own deadlines and process, while building relationships with the alumni, recruiters and classmates who can open a door, on top of a full course load.

How students solve it today

A search is spread across at least seven places: a spreadsheet or job tracker, email, LinkedIn, WhatsApp, iMessage, job boards and careers sites, and school platforms such as Handshake or 12twenty (Figure 2). The student is the integration layer: they search each board, look up who works at each target and who is an alum, retype what matters, decide from memory what to do next and prepare each coffee chat from scattered notes.

What the current process costs

  • Time. A career office advises first-year MBAs to spend 15–25 hours a week on recruiting, likening it to a part-time job (S1, Section 7), while two-thirds of business students in a 2017 survey reported 4–9 (S2). Yale SOM's list of organisations that hired its students in one year has 272 names (S7).
  • Attention. The useful signal is a small share of the noise. On the author's own accounts, the app's local filter (counting only, nothing sent) passed only 29 of 150 recent Gmail emails, 23 of 125 WhatsApp conversations and 3 of 36 iMessage conversations as touching his targets, his contacts or recruiting (EVIDENCE.md, Oct 25). A student reads all of it to find that part (one student's accounts: the share, not the minutes).
  • Quality. A follow-up promised in a chat, a deadline moved in a recruiter's email, or an alum on the team who was never found is easy to miss when no tool sees all of it. How often this happens is not measured (Section 7).
  • Money. Each paid tool covers one piece for $29 to $40 a month: Teal+ $29 per 30 days, Simplify+ $39.99, LinkedIn Premium Career $39.99, Huntr Pro $40 (vendor pages, read Oct 26, 2026). Their pages describe no reading of WhatsApp or iMessage.

Why it matters, and why one place

Recruiting runs on two clocks: hard application deadlines and soft relationship timing (a thank-you within a day, a promised résumé, a contact who offered to flag an application). A missed follow-up or a deadline noticed two days late cannot be bought back. The pieces are not the hard part; the joins are. Only a tool that holds the openings, the people and the conversations together can see that the alum on a team page, a classmate's intro on WhatsApp and a recruiter's email about a new deadline concern the same internship, and put it at the top of this week, while every message stays in the student's hands.

3What is new here

In one line: others do one piece (Simplify finds jobs and reads Gmail, Dex remembers people across WhatsApp and email, ChatGPT answers anything if you design the workflow); this app does the whole journey in one place, from finding the internship to preparing the coffee chat, with a quote behind every fact and without ever sending a word.

What this app does differently, and why it matters

The strongest rival is a do-it-yourself stack: ChatGPT connected to Gmail (its Work agent can run on a schedule or when an email arrives), Dex for WhatsApp and email (with MCP access for AI assistants), Simplify for jobs and a spreadsheet to hold it together, up to about $80 a month in paid tiers (ChatGPT Plus $20, Dex Premium $20, Simplify+ $39.99). Even wired together, the pages we read describe no posting confirmed on its own page, no person kept only when a public page names them, no filtering of chats on the student's computer before they leave it (23 of the author's 125 WhatsApp conversations passed), no person tied to an internship and its deadline, and no fact checked against its quote; ChatGPT and Simplify can act for the student (Dex on its Professional plan), and the student must build and maintain the workflow.

Being the single place is the product. An internship found on the web, the alum on its team, a classmate's offer to introduce and the recruiter's email that moves the deadline become one card with its people, its next step and the quote behind each fact, ranked against everything else this week. A stack holds the pieces; it cannot see that they belong together. Three choices make this safe to hand to a student: evidence (every fact keeps its exact quote, checked in code; every web result is re-read on its own page), read-only (one wrong message to a recruiter costs more than any time saved) and scope (the coach only ranks actions code derived from the records). Nor is it an overdone idea in disguise: no résumé review, no inbox summaries, no chat over documents; each text becomes typed records in a pipeline that persists. Where we are weaker: no résumé or autofill tools, far less job data than LinkedIn or Simplify (we never read LinkedIn, Handshake or 12twenty), alumni only from public pages, and chat syncing needs a Mac.

Organisation and delegation

The Career coach is the boss for planning and chat. It never reads the database: it may only rank the candidate actions that code derives from the records, and it learns about them by asking the two specialists through tools, at most 4 questions per request. Each specialist answers from the 10 records that best match the question (BM25) and may cite only ids it was given; specialists cannot delegate, so delegation is one level deep and cannot loop. Every other entry point is routed in code at zero tokens: the preferences form writes the profile every agent reads; text goes through the shield to the Inbox organizer; Find openings goes to the scout; Key people, doors and call prep go to Network intelligence. Agents only propose; the Records keeper writes. Where specialization did not pay we cut: one Inbox organizer call per text replaced two specialists.

Model and why

All five agents run on gpt-6-luna through the course's Portkey key: the key allows no other model, and gpt-6-luna is the low-cost tier ($0.10 per million input tokens, $0.01 cached, $0.50 output). Web search is OpenAI's built-in tool, which gpt-6-luna calls through the same key (first test, Oct 26: 8 searches and a cited answer in 12.0 s), so no second key or search vendor is needed. The work suits a small model: short, retrieved context and typed answers that code validates. Every agent runs with reasoning effort “low” and an 8,000-token cap per answer, and an answer from any other model is rejected (Portkey answers as gpt-6-luna-global; tested).

Every capability, following the student's journey

1Tell it what you want

A new account opens with the preferences form (Figure 4a). Code turns each target into a card and writes the answers at the top of the profile every agent reads, replacing an older version rather than stacking it (zero tokens; tested). 2Find

Search the whole web sends the Opportunity scout to OpenAI's web search with the profile and an optional focus. It proposes up to 8 internships open to MBA students, preferring organisations not tracked yet, each with its page's link and a sentence from it; the Records keeper opens every page itself, through the shield, and saves a result only if the page shows the role and names the organisation (Figure 5). The other button searches the public job boards of tracked organisations: on Oct 26 the scout searched 8 and saved 1 real posting, Robinhood Ventures' Investment Analyst MBA Intern (run 924006932786, 18.2 s, $0.00076).

3Shortlist and people

Every opportunity is judged against the profile: a fit grade and its reason on the card. On any card, Key people and alumni asks Network intelligence to search the open web for the team behind the role, the recruiting lead and alumni of the student's school; a person is saved as To contact, linked to the card, only if a public page names them (checked in code), and flagged as an alum when the agent reads that on the page (its instructions; code does not check the school). Who can open the door looks at the student's own people instead and proposes warm paths, with what to ask and a first message the student sends.

4Stay current

The dashboard is three views of one set of records: the Opportunities and Networking boards (Figure 7) and the Action plan, derived from both in code. It stays current without retyping: the Coach Connector, a small script on the student's Mac, reads Gmail, Messages and WhatsApp Desktop read-only and, with auto-sync on, checks every 30 minutes and sends only new messages that matter; LinkedIn and WhatsApp exports, emails (.eml) and calendar invites (.ics) are uploaded, the rest pasted (Figure 2). Each text takes one Inbox organizer call (3.6 to 9.3 s in Table 2), and the Records keeper merges it into the same cards.

6Safety

The app reads text written by strangers (recruiters, web pages, job boards), looks up real people on the web and holds a student's private relationships. The risks that matter would hurt the student or the people found: a hidden instruction changing a plan, a made-up fact or person, a message sent in the student's name, data reaching someone else, a stranger's details stored, a runaway bill. Each defence is enforced in code, not just asked for in a prompt, except where a row says so.

Tests, a live check and two adversarial reviews

  • 81 automated tests pass with no API key and no tokens (fake models stand in for gpt-6-luna; tests/, rerun after the web features on Oct 26), among them: injected instructions never reach the model and cannot act; web results are saved only when their page confirms them; key people need a public page that names them; no agent has a tool that could send anything; one student cannot see another's records; a looping agent is stopped; web searches have their own daily cap.
  • Live full check, 19 of 19 passed on the deployed site (Oct 26 morning, before the web features; runs/2026-10-26_live_full_check.json): every agent answered with real tokens; the shield, the link reader and the guardrails (422, 404, 401, daily budget) worked as designed.
  • Two adversarial reviews, with skeptics who tried to refute every finding: auto-sync, 18 findings confirmed (13 distinct), all fixed with regression tests; agent team, among others a paused intake still acknowledged to the connector (emails could be lost), all fixed.

Honest limits. The shield is pattern matching, so an instruction worded in a new way could reach the model, and pages read by OpenAI's search are not shielded at all. Hence the layers: in the injection test, a fake model that fully obeys the attacker is still blocked, because the keeper accepts only what the source literally says. The worst case is a wrong card the student can see and correct; nothing can be sent. A convincing but false posting will get a card, with the quote and link that show where it came from.

7Impact

The app does not only speed up chores: it makes practical a weekly search few students could sustain by hand. Every opening at every target, the people and alumni at each, a tracker that stays current and every conversation prepared take, by hand, 6.6–21.6 hours a week for a focused list of 40 organisations, 16–54% of a 40-hour week (a career office compares recruiting to “an additional class or part-time job”, S1), and 15.8–45.8 hours for a weekly sweep of the 272 organisations that hired Yale SOM MBAs (S7), 40–115%: a full-time job only at the high end. In the app: 1.8–5.5 and 2.5–7.6 hours estimate. 8Limits and next steps

What does not work yet, and current limitations

  • No real user test yet. Section 7's hours are an estimate: 31 of its 59 inputs are author assumptions (19 minutes, 12 weekly volumes), and the app's reach across the 272 organisations is not measured. The first tester will be the author, who is also the builder; one tester is not a sample, so classmates should follow.
  • “The whole web” is narrower than it sounds: OpenAI's web search plus four public job-board APIs and the careers pages that allow reading. LinkedIn, Handshake, 12twenty and Symplicity are never read, because their terms forbid it, so their postings and profiles must be pasted. Pages that need JavaScript, or whose robots.txt says no, cannot be confirmed, and the check is literal, so real postings can be set aside; 2 of 4 live results were not confirmed (the NBA's page did not contain the name as given).
  • Alumni are found only on public pages, and the alum flag is the agent's reading of the page: code confirms the page names the person, not where they studied. At DICK'S none was found; the school's alumni directory is not read, so the app can only point to it.
  • Web costs are capped per day, not per request. Prompts ask for at most 4 searches (2 in chat), but one request made 8; the coach's chat and call prep count toward the 20-a-day cap but are not stopped by it; search fees have no site-wide cap yet. Call prep's web search is in the code but not yet captured in a run.
  • Auto-sync of chats is half verified: Gmail syncs on its own; iMessage and WhatsApp wait for Full Disk Access for the scheduled Python (the refusal was verified, a real grant was not). It needs a Mac; Slack, LinkedIn and Android texts stay manual.
  • Free hosting: Render sleeps after 15 idle minutes (cold start 32 s) and the keep-awake Action ran only 4 times on Oct 25–26 (runs/2026-10-26_keep_awake_runs.txt); Supabase pauses after a week idle. Job boards are found by guessing the board's name.
  • Gaps: the job-board button always searches the 8 newest open targets (Table 5 assumes a rotating list). Portkey's fee is unknown and monthly use an estimate; retrieval recall and the chat filter's misses are unmeasured; still open in the interface, the date chip in Figure 9a and two Robinhood cards (target list and scout).

What we would build next with another month

  1. Measure. Run the protocol with 5 to 10 classmates and replace the assumed minutes of Table 5 with timed ones.
  2. An evaluation set: 50 fictional messages and postings and a set of web searches with known answers, scoring extraction, recall, the chat filter, web confirmations (right ones kept, real ones set aside) and plan quality on every change.
  3. Enforce the web budget in code: a cap on searches per request, the daily cap on chat and call prep too, and a site-wide budget for search fees.
  4. One-click connection: a signed Mac menu-bar app instead of a Terminal script, with Full Disk Access verified; Gmail through Google's sign-in after Google's review.
  5. A career-office pilot: always-on hosting ($32 a month), school sign-in and, with the school's permission, its own postings and alumni directory, which would close the two biggest gaps above.
